The Council should select the Cedar system because it is the only pilot configuration that meets the audited minimum capacity requirement, despite the higher operating costs, the mandatory inlet upgrade, and the significant uncertainty regarding winter reliability.

### Supporting Evidence: Capacity Constraints

The primary decision driver is the audited daily capacity, which creates a binary threshold for selection. The trial data confirms that Cedar delivers 1200 liters per day, satisfying the council requirement of at least 1000 liters per day [trial.md](file:///data/trial.md). In contrast, the independent 30-day audit corrected an early brochure claim that Birch delivered 1400 liters per day, establishing the true capacity at 900 liters per day [audit.md](file:///data/audit.md). Since the Birch system operates below the 1000-liter threshold, it fails the capacity requirement on its own [trial.md](file:///data/trial.md).

### Complicating Evidence: Cost, Energy, and Maintenance

Selecting Cedar introduces significant financial and operational trade-offs. While Birch operates at 12 credits per day, Cedar requires a 18 credits per day operating cost and necessitates a 400-credit inlet upgrade [cost.md](file:///data/cost.md). Additionally, Cedar consumes 6 kWh per day compared to Birch's 3 kWh per day [trial.md](file:///data/trial.md). Maintenance frequency also diverges, with Cedar requiring filter inspection every 7 days versus Birch's 14-day interval [maintenance.md](file://maintenance.md).

The routing diagram on page 2 of the pilot document visually distinguishes these operational paths. The blue route represents the normal treatment path through the UV chamber, while the orange route is explicitly labeled as a maintenance bypass that skips the UV chamber entirely [attachment://9a503717ac5a/page/2](attachment://9a503717ac5a/page/2). This bypass capability is a structural feature of the system design but does not alter the capacity requirements for the standard operating mode.

### Resolution: Reconciling Constraints

The evidence reveals a clear hierarchy of constraints where capacity is the hard limit. The audit reconciled the ledger and trial data, showing that Cedar alone is the only system meeting the 1000-liter/day requirement [audit.md](file://audit.md). The trade-off is between Cedar's higher cost and reliable capacity versus Birch's lower cost but insufficient capacity. The operating ledger does not capture the 400-credit inlet upgrade needed for Cedar, which must be factored into the budget [cost.md](file://cost.md).

### Prescription

The Council must select the Cedar system to ensure the minimum capacity is met, but must budget for the 400-credit inlet upgrade and accept the higher energy and maintenance costs. The alternative of using Birch alone is infeasible due to the 900-liter capacity shortfall.

### Conclusion

The Cedar system is the only viable option given the capacity constraint, though the decision carries financial and reliability risks. The Council must proceed with Cedar but must explicitly account for the inlet upgrade cost and the lack of long-term reliability data.

### Limitations

The pilot evidence explicitly states that winter performance was not tested [maintenance.md](file://content/maintenance.md). Neither system has established annual reliability from the 30-day trial, meaning winter reliability remains unknown [maintenance.md](file://content/maintenance.md).
