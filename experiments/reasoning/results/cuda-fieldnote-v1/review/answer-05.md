# The Capacity Constraint Dictates Harbor's Water Pilot Choice

## Thesis

Cedar must be selected despite its higher operating costs because it is the only system meeting the council's non-negotiable 1000-liter daily capacity requirement, while Birch fails this threshold at 900 liters per day. The capacity constraint renders cost and maintenance tradeoffs secondary to the fundamental service guarantee.

## Supporting Evidence: Capacity as the Deciding Factor

The trial results establish a hard boundary: Cedar delivers 1200 liters per day while Birch delivers only 900 liters per day, both measured under identical operating conditions [trial.md](file:///data/corpus/trial.md). The council's requirement of at least 1000 liters per day [audit.md](file:///data/corpus/audit.md) eliminates Birch from consideration regardless of other attributes. This finding is corroborated by an independent 30-day audit that corrected an early brochure claim of 1400 liters per day for Birch down to 900 liters per day, noting the brochure used a short clean-water test that is not representative [audit.md](file:///data/corpus/audit.md).

Cedar's capacity advantage is substantial—33% more water than Birch (1200 vs. 900 liters/day)—but comes at an energy cost: Cedar consumes 6 kWh per day versus Birch's 3 kWh per day [trial.md](file:///data/corpus/trial.md). Both systems met the drinking-water quality target during the trial, confirming that capacity and water quality are independently achievable.

## Complicating Evidence: Cost, Installation, and Maintenance Tradeoffs

Operating costs create a complicating tension. Cedar costs 18 credits per day to operate while Birch costs 12 credits per day, a 50% premium for Cedar [cost.md](attachment://cost.md). The inlet upgrade requirement further complicates the economics: Cedar requires a 400-credit upgrade to the inlet system, whereas Birch needs no upgrade [cost.md](attachment://cost.md). A grant covers installation but not recurring operating costs, meaning the council bears the full 18 credits/day for Cedar versus 12 credits/day for Birch.

Maintenance intervals offer Cedar a relative advantage: Cedar requires filter inspection every 7 days while Birch requires inspection every 14 days [maintenance.md](file:///content/maintenance.md). This represents a 50% reduction in maintenance frequency for Cedar, though the actual labor and replacement costs remain unknown.

## Resolution: Capacity Compliance Overrides Economic Tradeoffs

The resolution rests on a single constraint: the council's 1000-liter daily requirement is a hard floor, not a target. Birch's 900 liters/day falls short by 100 liters, a 10% gap that cannot be bridged by operational adjustments. The brochure's inflated 1400-liter claim for Birch was an artifact of testing conditions not representative of actual operation; the audit's correction to 900 liters/day confirms Birch cannot meet the requirement [audit.md](file:///data/corpus/audit.md).

Cedar's higher energy consumption (6 kWh/day) and operating cost (18 credits/day) are quantifiable tradeoffs, but they do not affect capacity compliance. The inlet upgrade cost (400 credits) is a one-time installation expense, whereas the operating cost difference (6 credits/day) compounds over time. The grant covering installation but not operations means the council must budget for the upgrade and the ongoing premium, but Cedar remains the only system that can deliver the promised service volume.

The maintenance advantage for Cedar—inspections every 7 days versus every 14 days—further tilts the balance toward Cedar, assuming maintenance labor costs are comparable across sites. This frequency difference suggests Cedar's filters may be more robust or require less frequent replacement, though the actual cost impact remains unknown.

## Prescription: Select Cedar With Budgeted Upgrade and Operating Premium

The council should select Cedar and budget for the 400-credit inlet upgrade plus 18 credits/day operating cost. The 6 kWh/day energy consumption must be factored into long-term utility projections, though the trial does not specify energy pricing [trial.md](file:///data/corpus/trial.md). The council must also plan for 7-day filter inspections and ensure sufficient budget for replacement cycles.

Birch should be rejected not because it is cheaper or easier to maintain, but because it cannot meet the fundamental service requirement. The 12 credits/day operating cost advantage is irrelevant when the system delivers 100 liters/day less than the council requires.

## Conclusion

Cedar must be selected because it is the only system meeting the council's 1000-liter daily capacity requirement; Birch's 900 liters/day falls short of the minimum [audit.md](file:///data/corpus/audit.md). The capacity constraint renders cost and maintenance tradeoffs secondary to the fundamental service guarantee. The council should budget for the 400-credit inlet upgrade and the 18 credits/day operating premium, accepting the higher energy consumption (6 kWh/day) as the price of reliable capacity delivery [trial.md](file:///data/corpus/trial.md).

### Limitations

The pilot evidence has critical gaps that limit confidence in long-term performance. First, the 30-day trial measured no winter performance; winter conditions were not tested [maintenance.md](file:///content/maintenance.md). Second, neither system has established annual reliability from this short pilot; the trial figures refer to the same operating conditions, not annual forecasts [trial.md](file:///data/corpus/trial.md). Third, the inlet upgrade cost (400 credits) and energy cost conversion (kWh to credits) remain unknown, as do the actual maintenance labor and replacement costs for the 7-day inspection cycle [cost.md](attachment://cost.md). The brochure correction process itself demonstrates that initial claims may be unreliable, suggesting additional validation may be needed before final deployment.
