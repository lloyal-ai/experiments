# Harbor Water Pilot Selection: Capacity Compliance as the Deciding Factor

The council must select the Cedar system because it is the only system that meets the council's minimum capacity requirement of 1000 liters per day, while Birch alone fails this threshold at 900 liters per day despite its lower energy consumption and longer maintenance intervals. This capacity compliance is the binary gate that determines feasibility; all other factors—energy, maintenance, and reliability—become secondary considerations once the basic requirement is satisfied.

## Capacity Compliance: The Binary Gate

The 30-day trial measured Cedar at 1200 liters per day and Birch at 900 liters per day, both under identical operating conditions [Trial results](trial.md). The independent 30-day audit corrected an early brochure claim that Birch delivered 1400 liters per day to the measured 900 liters per day, noting that the brochure's "short clean-water test was not representative" [Independent correction](audit.md). The council requires at least 1000 liters per day, so Birch alone fails the capacity requirement [Independent correction](audit.md).

This creates a hard constraint: Cedar qualifies (1200 > 1000 liters/day), while Birch does not (900 < 1000 liters/day). The 33% capacity gap between the systems (1200 vs. 900 liters/day) is not a matter of optimization but of feasibility—Birch cannot serve the council's minimum demand on its own.

## Energy Use: The Tradeoff

Cedar consumes 6 kWh per day, twice the energy use of Birch's 3 kWh per day [Trial results](trial.md). Both measurements were taken under the same operating conditions, though they do not represent annual forecasts [Trial results](trial.md). This energy difference represents a concrete tradeoff: Cedar delivers double the capacity but at double the energy cost.

The energy tradeoff becomes meaningful only after capacity compliance is secured. If capacity were not a hard constraint, Birch's lower energy use would be an attractive option. But since capacity is binary—either the system meets the requirement or it doesn't—the energy tradeoff is secondary to the fundamental question of whether the system can deliver water at all.

## Maintenance Frequency: Operational Burden

Cedar requires filter inspection every 7 days, while Birch requires it every 14 days [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md). This represents a 50% increase in maintenance frequency for Cedar. The pilot measured no winter performance, and neither system has established annual reliability from this short trial [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md).

The maintenance frequency difference creates operational complexity: Cedar demands more frequent filter changes and presumably more labor or resource allocation. However, this operational burden is manageable compared to the failure mode of selecting Birch—capacity shortfall. The maintenance tradeoff is a cost of doing business; the capacity failure is a system design flaw.

## Reliability: What Remains Unknown

The 30-day pilot measured no winter performance [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md). Winter performance was not measured in the trial [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md). The 30-day trial was conducted under specific operating conditions, not representative of annual performance [Trial results](trial.md). Neither system has established annual reliability from this short pilot [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md).

This reliability uncertainty complicates the decision. The trial results apply only to the specific operating conditions tested, not to annual performance or winter conditions. The brochure's Birch claim of 1400 liters per day was based on a "short clean-water test that is not representative" [Independent correction](audit.md). The council must recognize that the 30-day results are not guarantees of annual performance.

## Prescription: Select Cedar, Acknowledge Winter Unknowns

The council should select Cedar because it is the only system that meets the capacity requirement. The energy tradeoff (6 kWh/day vs. 3 kWh/day) and maintenance burden (7-day vs. 14-day filter inspections) are secondary considerations. The reliability uncertainty—particularly regarding winter performance—remains an open question that should be monitored but does not negate the fundamental feasibility gap.

## Conclusion

The thesis holds: Cedar is the only viable option because capacity compliance is a binary requirement that Birch cannot meet. The energy and maintenance tradeoffs are manageable operational costs; the reliability uncertainty is a known limitation of the short pilot but does not change the fundamental feasibility assessment. Selecting Birch would fail the basic requirement; selecting Cedar meets it at the cost of higher energy use and more frequent maintenance.

### Limitations

The findings do not establish annual reliability for either system. The 30-day trial was conducted under specific operating conditions, not representative of annual performance [Trial results](trial.md). Winter performance was not measured in the trial [Maintenance and limitations](maintenance.md). The council must recognize that Cedar's capacity performance may vary outside the tested conditions, and the reliability gap remains unaddressed.
